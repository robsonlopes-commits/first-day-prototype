# First Day copy

Copy is one of the most important assets at First Day. It is **not a template system**: there is no fixed structure to fill in. There are principles, a way of working and a set of checks. Use judgment, and let the idea decide the shape.

Inputs to pull in before writing (do not write from memory):
- `firstday-whats-new` for the angles being tested NOW (for example growth and the choline/teen brain story for Teens, hidden hunger as an invisible threat, methylfolate versus folic acid, right dose for the age). Prefer a live angle over a stock one. If the page has no angle, propose one or two and ask.
- `firstday-brand-knowledge` for facts, products, stats and which claims are approved (`references/science-and-claims.md`), and, in this skill, `references/live-pages-reference.md` for the voice of the live landing pages (inspiration only).
- The audience. Do not assume which demos (Kids, Toddlers, Teens, Women, Men) a page supports: ask (rule in `firstday-design`).

## What good First Day copy does
1. **Emotional first.** Start from a real moment the reader recognizes: the plate pushed away, homework that never ends, the meltdown that came from nowhere, the 3 pm crash, the night they cannot switch off. The reader should think "that is my house". The parent is never the villain (the food system, outdated vitamins and bad information are). "You are not doing it wrong."
2. **Direct response.** Every block has one job and moves the reader toward the action: a clear promise, a specific reason to believe it, proof, and a next step. Benefit before feature, concrete before abstract, short before long. Calls to action say what happens next in the reader's terms. Urgency only when it is real (a real deadline or gift threshold from the live offer).
3. **Science and numbers whenever they strengthen the point.** A fact the reader can repeat ("up to 40% of kids can't convert folic acid", "fresh produce has lost up to 75% of its nutrients", "the prefrontal cortex doesn't mature until the mid 20s") is more persuasive than an adjective. Pair the number with the plain translation of why it matters, and with where it comes from when the page needs credibility. Never invent a figure, never stack five stats in one block.
4. **Mechanism over assertion.** Do not just say a product helps. Show the chain: what is happening in the world or in the body, how that shows up at home, what the nutrient does about it. Cause to effect is more convincing and more emotional than a list of benefits.
5. **Live angles over stock angles.** Check what is being tested (growth for Teens, the teen brain under construction, choline, hidden hunger). A fresh angle with a built in reason to act now beats a generic one (for example "growth plates are open now, and they do not stay open forever").
6. **Compliant by construction.** "Supports", "helps", asterisk on functional claims, dagger on customer survey percentages, no diagnosing, curing or promising, no unapproved claims from the Bio-Matched concept without flagging them as pending. See the guardrails in `firstday-brand-knowledge`.

## Coherence rules (learned from the owner's corrections, see `references/owner-feedback-log.md`)
- **The content must deliver what the title promises.** If a section is called "What Is Happening Behind The Plate", every item must explain what is actually happening, with real mechanisms. Credibility badges, product features or generic benefits under that title do not belong. Re-read the title after drafting the body, and the body after changing the title.
- **Siblings share one logic.** Items in a list or cards in a run use the same perspective, tense, level of detail and kind of statement. If three cards describe a problem moment, the fourth cannot describe the ideal (Bedtime "should feel calm" among tense moments). Same type of heading (all causes, all moments, all outcomes), comparable length, same depth.
- **A title is a hook, not a label.** Prefer the recognition or the tension ("If You Have A Picky Eater At Home, This Is Probably Your Routine") over a category name ("A Day With A Picky Eater"). A title and its sections should read as one conversation.
- **The CTA continues the story.** The button answers the section it closes ("Meet The Solution" after a section about what is happening), instead of jumping to a product or a mechanic the reader has not asked for yet.
- **No filler, no duplicates.** Do not repeat a promo or reassurance that appears elsewhere on the page (a guarantee line under every button, a second "order by" note, decorative percent tiles, a generic "note to parents" that repeats the hero). Every line must earn its place.
- **Consistency across audiences.** When a block exists for several audiences, the structure of the story stays the same and the facts change per audience (use only audiences the owner confirmed).
- **Say it once, plainly.** One idea per sentence, no jargon without translation, no long paragraphs where a short line works. New copy has no hyphens, dashes, semicolons or colons (existing production copy stays as is). Emoji only in chips, badges and bullet leads, one at the start.

## Way of working
1. State the single job of the block and who it is for (one sentence, in your notes).
2. Pull the angle (`firstday-whats-new`) and the facts (`firstday-brand-knowledge`). Pick the one strongest fact, not all of them.
3. Draft the emotional hook first, then the mechanism, then the action. Try two or three different openings (a scene, a fact, a reframe) and pick the strongest, do not settle on the first.
4. Run the checks below. Fix before showing.
5. When you present copy, say in one line why it works and which angle and facts it uses, and flag anything pending or unverified. When the owner corrects copy, add the correction and the reason to `references/owner-feedback-log.md` (date, before, after, why) so the lesson sticks.

## Checks before saying done
- Does the body deliver every promise of its title? Does the title match the body?
- Do sibling items read as one set (same logic, tense, depth, length)?
- Is there a real moment, a real fact and a reason to act, or only adjectives?
- Is every number real, sourced and compliant (asterisk or dagger where needed)?
- Is anything repeated elsewhere on the page, or purely decorative?
- Does the button or the next line follow naturally from what was just said?
- Is it short, scannable and mobile first (read it at 375px)?
- Is it on the angle we want to test, and did you ask which audience?
