# Voice and humor: how Sesame Street writes

What is sourced is marked. What is craft advice drawn from those facts is marked **(craft)**. Every example line in this file is **invented for this guide, not a quote** from the show.

## The mechanics of the show's language
- **The child is addressed directly.** Since Season 56 characters speak **to camera** and episodes are built to be **played along** with (Sesame Workshop press release, 2025). In Elmo's World, **Elmo leads the child through the action instead of an adult narrator**; the segment was described as "a playdate between the child and Elmo" (Wikipedia, citing Kevin Clash). **(craft)** On a page: the character talks to the reader as a friend, not about the reader.
- **Repetition is a teaching tool.** Elmo's World "relied heavily on repetition", with the same structure in every episode (Wikipedia). **(craft)** Repeat the key word three times across a page (the product's one benefit, the one number) and keep a recurring bit (a character's catchphrase) in the same place on every section.
- **One topic per scene, seen from a three-year-old's side.** Elmo's World topics were things like balls and dancing, "from a three-year-old's perspective". **(craft)** Start from what the child notices (the broccoli looks like a tree), not from the nutrient.
- **Names and numbers are things to play with.** The Count turns counting into an event (thunder and lightning); Big Bird mispronounces names and sings the alphabet as one word; Grover avoids contractions. **(craft)** Give every character one verbal tic and keep it consistent.
- **Name the feeling, then give a small step.** Seasons 55 to 57 teach children to name big feelings and calm down (the "heart breath", memory boxes, apologizing, sharing). **(craft)** A bedtime or tantrum page can follow the same two beats: "that feeling is big" then "here is one small thing to try".
- **Short, concrete and warm.** Present tense, simple verbs, concrete nouns, no jargon without a plain translation. Sentences of one idea. Exclamation marks sparingly, they belong to Elmo and Cookie Monster, not to the whole page.

## Two levels of humor (co-viewing)
- The show was designed from the start to **keep parents and older siblings in the room**, a goal the producers called **co-viewing**: humor, cultural references and celebrities for the adults, so that children learn more with someone watching with them (Wikipedia, Smithsonian and Yahoo quotes from the current executive producer; one source says 80% of parents co-watched by 2019). Monsterpiece Theater (Cookie Monster as "Alistair Cookie") is the classic example: a children's gag on the surface, a Masterpiece Theatre parody underneath. Former writer Norman Stiles said bits often had two sides, one for kids and one for adults (WGA East interview).
- **Dissent worth knowing:** some critics feel the parent-wink can crowd out the child. Keep the child layer genuinely childlike and make the adult layer a small aside, never the point.
- **(craft) For First Day pages:** the child layer is the character's want and sound (Cookie Monster's "Me want...", Grover's earnest rescue). The parent layer is one gentle line that only a parent gets (the 3 pm crash, the fourth "one more story", the plate returning full). Parents are never the villain.

## What makes a Sesame line work (checklist)
1. Could a four-year-old say or understand it? Read it aloud.
2. Does it use the character's grammar (below)?
3. Is the joke **gentle**: a mistake, a mix-up, a funny sound, an opposite? Never a put-down.
4. Does it model something kind, curious or calm?
5. Is there one real thing (a fact, a moment) underneath the play?

## Character voice cheatsheet
| Character | Grammar and sound | Invented example |
|---|---|---|
| **Elmo** | Third person ("Elmo"), never "I". Giggle, questions, short bursts | "Elmo has a question. What is hiding behind the broccoli? Ha ha!" |
| **Cookie Monster** | "Me" instead of "I". Loud, hungry, big-hearted. Pairs "want" with a food | "Me want to try new food today. Me hungry for learning!" (only with a real food message) |
| **The Count** | Formal greeting, announces each number, staccato laugh, thunder at the total | "One bottle! Two gummies a day! Thirty days! Ah ah ah!" |
| **Grover** | No contractions, over-formal, super-enthusiastic, clumsy | "I am here to help! I do not know how, but I am here!" |
| **Oscar** | Grouchy, dismissive, scram, but tender to children and honest about feelings | "I do not like anything nice. Scram. Okay, maybe that smoothie is not terrible." |
| **Big Bird** | Simple, sincere, slow, mixes up names, asks plain questions | "Why do we need vitamins? I do not know. Let us find out together." |
| **Abby Cadabby** | Sparkly, wide-eyed, "That's so magic!", "gotta poof" | "That is so magic! I just learned what is inside my gummy." |
| **Bert / Ernie** | Bert exasperated and precise, Ernie bubbly and oblivious | Ernie: "Bert, what if we have dessert first?" Bert: "Ernie, no." |
| **Telly** | Earnest worrier, lists his worries, comforted by friends | "What if the dinner is too green? Telly is a little worried." |

Do not copy any real catchphrase beyond the short titles listed here; never quote song lyrics. For anything not listed, **ask**.

## Tone rules
**Do:** warm, silly, curious, kind; friendship; trying; naming feelings; one clear action at the end; credit the grown-up as the hero who is "trying again tomorrow".
**Do not:** be scary (Snuffy's puppet was redesigned because the sunken eyes frightened children), shame, nag, bribe (a gummy is not a reward), joke about the child's body or weight, say "good food / bad food" for the child to carry (the show's wording is **anytime and sometimes foods**), use sarcasm at the child, make a medical promise through a character, use a character to **diagnose** ("you might have hidden hunger" is a no).

## Combining with First Day copy rules
- New copy keeps First Day's rules: title case titles, no widows, no dashes, colons or semicolons, emoji only in chips, one idea per sentence.
- Dialogue lines can use quotation marks. A character's name is capitalized (Cookie Monster, the Count). Stage directions live in the page structure (badges, captions), not in long narration.
- Claims stay outside the character's mouth when they carry an asterisk: the character introduces, the plain First Day text with its asterisk delivers.
